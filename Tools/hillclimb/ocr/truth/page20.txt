The Socratic paradox οὐδεὶς ἑκὼν ἁμαρτάνει, that no one does wrong willingly, has been explained in many ways. Some take ἁμαρτάνειν to mean intellectual error rather than moral fault; others point out that ἀρετή and ἐπιστήμη are for Socrates practically identical, so that to know the good is already to be good. Neither explanation is wholly satisfactory, but both are preferable to the view that Socrates was merely playing upon words.
The Heraclitean saying πάντα ῥεῖ, everything flows, does not occur in this form in any ancient text of Heraclitus himself, though Plato in the Cratylus attributes to him a very similar thought. The genuine fragments speak rather of the river into which one cannot step twice, and of the strife, ἔρις, that is justice, δίκη. The tag is therefore a summary made by later writers, and it should be cited as such.
Aristotle’s definition of man as a political animal, ὁ ἄνθρωπος φύσει πολιτικὸν ζῷον, is often quoted as though it were a statement about human sociability in general. The context in the Politics shows that it is nothing of the kind. The πόλις is for him a particular form of association, and only within it can the capacity for speech, λόγος, and for the perception of justice and injustice, δίκαιον and ἄδικον, be fully exercised.
Herodotus describes his work as an ἱστορίης ἀπόδεξις, a display of inquiry, and the word ἱστορία has in his usage not yet acquired the restricted sense of a narrative of past events. It means the process of finding out, by asking and looking; and the same root appears in the verb ἱστορεῖν, which Herodotus uses of travellers who question the priests of Egypt about their sacred records.
The word λόγος is among the hardest in the Greek language to render, for it means at once speech, reason, account, and proportion.
