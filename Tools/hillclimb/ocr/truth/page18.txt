STUDIES IN GREEK THOUGHT
The word λόγος is among the hardest in the Greek language to render, for it means at once speech, reason, account, and proportion. In Heraclitus it seems to denote the common law that governs the changes of the world; in Plato it is most often the explanation which a man can give of what he knows; and in the opening verse of the Fourth Gospel, ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, it is the divine Word through which all things came to be.¹
Aristotle’s definition of man as a political animal, ὁ ἄνθρωπος φύσει πολιτικὸν ζῷον, is often quoted as though it were a statement about human sociability in general. The context in the Politics shows that it is nothing of the kind. The πόλις is for him a particular form of association, and only within it can the capacity for speech, λόγος, and for the perception of justice and injustice, δίκαιον and ἄδικον, be fully exercised.²
The Socratic paradox οὐδεὶς ἑκὼν ἁμαρτάνει, that no one does wrong willingly, has been explained in many ways. Some take ἁμαρτάνειν to mean intellectual error rather than moral fault; others point out that ἀρετή and ἐπιστήμη are for Socrates practically identical, so that to know the good is already to be good. Neither explanation is wholly satisfactory, but both are preferable to the view that Socrates was merely playing upon words.³
The inscription at Delphi, γνῶθι σεαυτόν, was understood by the Greeks as a warning against presumption, a reminder that the human being is mortal, θνητός, and must not claim the privileges of the gods, θεοί. Only later, in the schools of philosophy, did the precept come to mean an invitation to look inward at the ψυχή. The change of sense is a good example of how a famous phrase may be made to serve the purposes of each succeeding age.
